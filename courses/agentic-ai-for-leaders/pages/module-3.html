Delegation and Boundaries

Dashboard — The Boundary Drawing Board

Given that autonomy is accepted, where should authority sit inside the workflow?

Leadership move · Design
Opening context

Where exactly is the line?

In Module 2 most of you landed somewhere in the middle: the agent acts here, but not there. That answer is a starting position, not a final position. Partial autonomy is where most risk lives, because it is rarely clear where the boundary actually sits. The moment you say “partial”, the next questions get harder: where exactly is the line? Which decisions sit on which side of it? And what happens when the line gets pushed under pressure? This module is about drawing the line — and testing whether it will hold.

Concept layer 1

Authority is not binary

A common mistake is to treat delegation as a yes-or-no question. In reality, authority exists on a spectrum — and each position carries a different accountability load.

ExecuteCarries out a decision that has already been made.
RecommendProposes; a human decides.
Decide within limitsMakes the call inside explicit boundaries.
ExcludedPlays no role in this decision at all.

Which is why any AI workflow contains three types of decisions:

Delegable decisionsRoutine, reversible and lower consequence.
Decisions with constraintsMay be delegated only within defined limits.
Non-delegable decisionsAI may inform the process, but responsibility remains explicitly human.
Key message

The question is not “Can the AI do this?” The question is: “Should the authority be delegated?”

Case study

The Complaint Triage Workflow, revisited

Same agent, same pilot data. But this time you are not judging the workflow — you are judging the decisions inside it. Which should remain human-owned, and which, if any, could be assigned to the agent within clear limits?

Before you make the call, study what the three options actually mean in practice, using one task — “send an acknowledgement to the complainant”:

Delegate
The agent may decide when and how to perform the task and execute it without prior human approval — humans monitor performance rather than approve each action. The agent receives a complaint, generates an acknowledgement using the complaint context and sends it immediately.
Delegate with constraints
The agent may execute without case-by-case approval, but only inside explicitly defined rules, permissions and escalation thresholds. It sends an approved template only after confirming the complaint was recorded; it may personalise basic details but cannot admit fault, promise compensation, state an outcome, or respond to sensitive or high-risk complaints — those go to a human.
Do not delegate
The agent has no authority to communicate directly with the complainant — a human must approve or perform the action. It may prepare a draft, but a complaints officer must review and send it — or the agent does not participate in this step at all.
Activity 3.1≈ 25 min

Where Is Your Line?

The Authority & Accountability Boundary Map. This is not a workflow design exercise — it is a leadership decision about what authority is delegated, what remains human, and what you are prepared to stand behind. For each of the eight decisions, make the call: Delegate, delegate with Constraint, or Non-delegable. Do it on your own first, then compare with your table. Not what the system can do — what you are willing to allow and stand behind.

D — Delegate to the agentC — Delegate with ConstraintN — Non-delegable (a human must make this call)

Per decision, ask yourself: if this went wrong, would I be willing to defend allowing the agent to make this decision?

Delegate. Constrain. Protect.

Some decisions are routine and easier to delegate. Some can only be delegated with constraints. Some carry such consequence that responsibility must remain explicitly human. That is why a defensible boundary has three categories rather than two.

A defensible boundary has three parts: what you delegate, what you refuse to delegate — and what should never have been treated as a delegation question at all. Most leaders focus only on the first.

Additional considerations
Remember

A boundary is only real if it can be understood, applied, and defended. If it cannot, it is not a boundary — it is an assumption.

Concept layer 2 — boundary design

The four parts of a defensible boundary

Drawing the line is only the beginning. A defensible boundary must be explicit enough for others to implement, monitor and defend. The three design tools — permissions, thresholds and constraints — combine into four parts:

1AuthorityWhat is the agent allowed to do — execute, recommend, or decide within limits?
2ThresholdsHow far may it go before a human becomes involved? Numerical, policy or risk limits that define the edge.
3Escalations & constraintsUnder what conditions must authority return to a human?
4OwnershipWho remains accountable — at decision, operational and authority level? Ownership must stay clear even when the work is no longer done by a person.
Key message

Delegation does not remove accountability. A defensible boundary answers: what can the agent do — and who owns what happens when it does?

Protecting human judgement

Boundaries fail through dozens of small nudges

Every boundary you draw today will be tested tomorrow by efficiency pressure. The team is overwhelmed. Volumes spike. The agent could do a little more. That is how boundaries fail — not through one decision, through dozens of small nudges. Your responsibility is not only to draw the boundary; it is to protect it when the organisation forgets why it existed.

And remember the value consideration: value doesn't always come from full autonomy. Sometimes it comes from better recommendations, faster triage, or clearer escalation. The leadership decision is not “how much can we delegate?” but “what level of delegation creates the most value with acceptable risk?” The over-delegation failure mode — more AI = better — is very common in reality.

Activity 3.2≈ 15 min with 3.3

Defensible Boundary Design

From the decisions you mapped in 3.1, choose one — focus on a Delegate with Constraint decision — and complete the full boundary design. Use the group conversation as a sounding board, but write the version you would personally be prepared to defend. The aim is not a perfect boundary; it is testing whether yours is clear and practical.

Study the worked example first — “send an acknowledgement to the complainant”
Decision
Delegate with Constraint
Authority
May send an approved acknowledgement after confirming the complaint was recorded; may include name, case number, receipt date and standard next steps. Cannot admit fault, assess merits, promise an outcome, offer compensation or alter required response timeframes.
Threshold
Automatic sending only when required information is complete, contact details verified, an approved template suits, and no high-risk indicators are detected.
Escalation
Stop and refer to a complaints officer on: vulnerability, distress, threats, safety concerns, legal or regulatory action, privacy issues, potential media interest, unclear category, missing information, low classification confidence, or a system conflict.
Ownership
Operational: Complaints Manager owns the workflow, templates, thresholds and performance. Technical: the AI/technology team maintains performance, access, integrations and logging. Risk/control: Risk, Legal, Privacy or Compliance approve controls and review incidents. Case: once escalated, a named complaints officer owns the case. Accountability: the designated business owner remains accountable — accountability does not transfer to the agent or the technology provider.
Activity 3.3≈ 15 min

Stress-Test the Boundary

A boundary is only useful if it holds under pressure. Apply three realistic pressures to the position you just designed. For each: would our boundary still hold? What would make it move? What would need to be in place to protect it? Could we still defend it if challenged?

Pressure 1 — Efficiency
It's six months from now. Your team is overwhelmed. Complaints are spiking. The agent could handle the cases you currently route to humans — if you just widened its remit by a small amount. The CEO is asking why response times have slipped.
Pressure 2 — Drift over time
It's twelve months from now. Nobody has consciously moved the boundary. But the agent's training data has shifted, the team has slowly expanded what it accepts, and the thresholds you set are still on paper while the agent's actual behaviour has crept beyond them.
Pressure 3 — Accountability challenge
It's two years from now. The boundary held. The agent operated inside the lines you set. But something has still gone wrong — a customer has suffered harm, and a regulator is asking how this happened.

Sample answers (Activity Workbook, p.36)

Efficiency pressure: “The boundary holds only if the team has enough capacity to take the medium and high cases. If we are overwhelmed, we expand the team, not the agent's remit. Written threshold, in policy, before the pressure hits.”

Drift over time: “Monthly review of cases the agent classified as low that later turned out to be medium or high. If the misclassification rate climbs above an agreed line, we pause the agent's classification autonomy until we understand why.”

Accountability challenge: “Yes. The boundary is in writing, the non-delegable items are documented, and the drift signals are monitored. The decision was deliberate.”

A boundary that survives efficiency pressure, drift and accountability challenge is more than a position on paper — it is a boundary the organisation can operate with. It signals what may be automated, what remains human, and what you are prepared to stand behind when challenged.

Activity 3.4≈ 15 minYour workflow

My Delegation Decision

Back to your own workflow. Identify the smallest piece of work you would be comfortable allowing an AI agent to perform. Not delegating as much as possible — a sensible starting point: one specific, observable task where the value is clear, the risk is manageable, and the human boundary can be stated. Not “manage onboarding” — think “check completeness”, “route a request”, “send an approved acknowledgement”.

High volume or clear rules aren't sufficient on their own — consider the consequence of error and whether the action is recoverable.
Would another person know exactly when escalation is required from what you've written?
Key message to land

Responsible delegation starts with the smallest useful task, a clear human boundary and an escalation trigger. Capability does not determine permission — leaders do.

Leadership conversation guide — where should autonomy stop?

Questions to take back to your team and vendors

Questions to ask my team
  • Which decisions should remain human-owned?
  • Which decisions are suitable for delegation?
  • Which decisions need constraints?
  • What authority are we actually transferring?
  • Who remains accountable?
Questions to ask a vendor
  • Can authority levels be adjusted?
  • Can escalation thresholds be configured?
  • How are exceptions handled?

Once you've decided what the agent may do and what stays human, three more questions open up: who owns this agent as a standing part of the organisation? What happens to the shape of the team once the routine work moves? And what do the people whose work just changed do now? Those aren't boundary questions — they're transformation questions, and Day 2 comes back to them.

Key takeaways

What Module 3 established

  • Authority is not binary — an agent may execute, recommend, decide within limits, or be excluded entirely, and each carries a different accountability load.
  • A defensible boundary has three categories: delegate, constrain, protect — and four parts: authority, thresholds, escalations and ownership.
  • Boundaries fail through dozens of small nudges, not one decision. Drawing the boundary and governing within it are different leadership tasks.
  • A policy states what the organisation believes; a boundary determines what the system actually does.
  • Refusing delegation is also a leadership decision — weigh the value lost as deliberately as the risk avoided.
The closing thought

A defensible boundary is not about what the AI can do. It is about what the organisation is prepared to stand behind when challenged.
