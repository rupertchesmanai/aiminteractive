MERIDIAN REGIONAL SERVICES — DRAFT THREE-YEAR SERVICE TRANSFORMATION STRATEGY (v0.9, internal working draft)

1. BACKGROUND AND CONTEXT
Meridian Regional Services (MRS) provides community-facing services across a mixed metropolitan and regional footprint, including customer service centres, phone-based support, field visits and a growing but fragmented set of digital channels. Over the past five years demand has grown approximately 22 per cent while the workforce has grown 6 per cent. Customer expectations have shifted markedly toward digital self-service for routine matters, while the complexity of the remaining assisted interactions has increased. Our current operating model was designed for a channel mix that no longer reflects reality: roughly 60 per cent of routine transactions still require staff mediation even though comparable organisations complete 80 per cent or more of equivalent transactions digitally. Meanwhile, our most vulnerable customers report difficulty navigating the very digital channels intended to free up staff time for them. This strategy proposes a phased transformation of how MRS designs, delivers and continuously improves its services over the next three years.

2. THE CASE FOR CHANGE
Four pressures make the status quo unsustainable. First, demand growth is outpacing funding growth, and modelling suggests a service capacity shortfall equivalent to 140 full-time staff by year three if nothing changes. Second, our customer satisfaction results are diverging: digital-confident customers rate us poorly against private-sector benchmarks, while customers who rely on assisted channels report longer waits year on year. Third, our technology estate carries significant accumulated risk — the core case-management platform is two major versions behind vendor support, and 14 discrete legacy applications perform overlapping functions. Fourth, staff engagement surveys show frontline teams feel caught between rising demand and tools that make simple work slow; regretted attrition in customer-facing roles reached 18 per cent last year.

3. STRATEGIC INTENT
By the end of year three, MRS will: (a) complete at least 75 per cent of routine transactions through digital self-service, measured quarterly; (b) redirect the staff time released — an estimated 90,000 hours annually — into complex casework, proactive outreach and support for customers who cannot or prefer not to use digital channels; (c) reduce average end-to-end resolution time for complex cases from 19 days to 10; and (d) retire at least 9 of the 14 legacy applications. The intent is explicitly not a headcount reduction program: released capacity is reinvested in higher-value service. This commitment should be communicated clearly and repeated often, because staff scepticism on this point is the single greatest cultural risk to the program.

4. PROGRAM ARCHITECTURE
The transformation comprises three streams. Stream One (Digital Front Door) consolidates our seven current entry points into a single account-based portal with assisted-digital support in every service centre, delivered in four releases beginning in month eight. Stream Two (Case Modernisation) upgrades the core case-management platform, standardises our 60 most common case types onto shared workflows, and introduces automated status notifications, cutting the estimated 30 per cent of inbound calls that are progress-chasing. Stream Three (Workforce and Ways of Working) retrains approximately 400 staff into blended roles combining assisted-digital coaching, complex casework and outreach, supported by a new capability framework, protected training time of four hours per fortnight, and revised quality measures that reward resolution rather than throughput.

5. SEQUENCING AND MILESTONES
Year one focuses on foundations: portal release one (account creation, the five highest-volume transactions), platform upgrade planning, workforce baseline and the first two service-centre pilots. Year two is the volume year: portal releases two and three, migration of the top 40 case types, retirement of the first six legacy applications, and retraining of the first 250 staff. Year three completes the migration, retires the remaining targeted applications, embeds continuous-improvement routines and transitions the program into business-as-usual ownership. Hard gates apply at months 12, 20 and 30; the program does not proceed through a gate unless customer-facing service levels have been maintained or improved for two consecutive quarters.

6. INVESTMENT AND RETURNS
Total program investment is estimated at $48m over three years, phased approximately 40/40/20. Benefits comprise avoided capacity shortfall (equivalent to $19m annually by year three), reduced technology run costs ($6.2m annually after legacy retirement), and reduced rework from failure demand (estimated at $4m annually, with lower confidence). Non-financial benefits include improved accessibility outcomes, faster support for vulnerable customers and improved staff retention. The benefits case deliberately excludes speculative revenue and counts only savings that can be traced to a specific operational change with a named owner.

7. RISKS AND MITIGATIONS
The principal risks are: digital exclusion of vulnerable customers (mitigated by assisted-digital support in every centre, retained phone and face-to-face channels, and co-design with community organisations); change fatigue in a workforce that has absorbed three restructures in six years (mitigated by protected training time, visible reinvestment of released capacity and a single consolidated change calendar); vendor delivery risk on the platform upgrade (mitigated by contractual gates aligned to program gates and a parallel-run fallback); and benefit leakage, where released time is absorbed invisibly rather than redirected (mitigated by quarterly capacity reconciliation reviews owned by the COO). A dedicated risk of over-automation is acknowledged: no case type will move to fully automated decision-making without human review during this strategy period, and any future proposal to do so requires a separate governance decision.

8. GOVERNANCE AND MEASUREMENT
The program reports to a Transformation Board chaired by the Chief Executive, meeting monthly, with delegated authority to a Program Steering Group for decisions under $500k. Each stream has a single accountable owner. Progress is measured against a published scorecard of twelve indicators spanning customer outcomes, staff experience, delivery and financials, reported quarterly and shared with all staff in full, including misses. An independent assurance review is commissioned before each hard gate. The strategy will be refreshed at the eighteen-month point, and any material change to scope, benefits or the workforce commitments in section 3 requires Board approval and staff consultation before adoption.
