Module 3

Better-Prepared Attendees

The same message lands differently for every person who receives it. This module uses AI to adapt tone, structure and language for real, diverse colleagues — while meaning, tone and context stay yours.

🕘 ~70 minutes · Activities 3.1 & 3.2
The evidence

High-performing teams are high-performance communicators.

Google's Project Aristotle found that communication clarity and conversational turn-taking were two of the strongest behavioural predictors of team effectiveness — particularly when combined with psychological safety. Teams that communicate clearly are better equipped to collaborate, identify risks, distribute workload and adapt to changing conditions.

Studies of cross-functional and hybrid teams show the same pattern: high performance goes with explicit communication norms, predictable information flow and shared interpretation of messages rather than individual assumptions. High-performing teams use closed-loop communication — messages are delivered, acknowledged and verified for shared meaning. Misunderstandings, missing context and tone mismatches are among the most common contributors to delays, conflict and rework.

The mechanics

Why communication needs differ — and how AI helps.

"Clear" to one person is overwhelming, vague or indirect to another. Common sources of mismatch: direct instructions vs narrative explanation, structured sequencing vs free-form ideas, visual vs text-heavy processing, language proficiency and cultural expectations, and cognitive-load factors like working memory, sensory load and pace.

Styles you'll practise transforming into

  • Direct — concise and instruction-oriented
  • Step-by-step — sequenced actions, no ambiguity
  • Structured — headings, grouping, clear segmentation
  • Visual — simplified explanations using shapes or spatial metaphors
  • Context-rich — additional framing and background
  • Warm — relational and supportive
  • Translated — adapted for language and cultural tone

Safeguards — non-negotiable

  • Check the AI hasn't added assumptions or removed important details.
  • Confirm tone remains appropriate for your audience.
  • Reinstate your own voice so the message remains yours.
  • Never rely on AI for sensitive or high-stakes communication without careful review.
Please note: the differences in communication styles below are represented through fictional personas, used to explore communication design choices — never to profile or assess real individuals.
Activity 3.1

Adapting messages for different communication styles

Rewrite the same message for different personas, using AI to support you — and watch how clarity, psychological safety and motivation shift with structure and tone.

Step 2 · Review the three example personas — choose one

Alex

Senior Business Analyst — the Structured Thinker

Alex prefers information that is organised, predictable and easy to scan — a consistent pattern with headings, bullet points and numbered steps. Narrative explanations, conversational tone or long paragraphs make it harder for Alex to extract what matters.

Key details, dependencies or decisions buried inside text are easily lost on Alex. They appreciate messages that get to the point quickly, outline actions clearly and define next steps without ambiguity.

Priya

Risk and Controls Specialist — the Detail-Oriented Analyst

Priya prefers communication that is meticulous, thorough and complete, with minimal missing information — context, assumptions, definitions and known risks included. When details are incomplete, she cannot progress confidently and has to follow up.

She responds well to communication that explains why something is happening, not only what. Oversimplified messages can feel careless to her; she prefers documentation that lets her validate facts independently.

Marcus

Customer Experience Lead — the Relational Collaborator

Marcus processes information best when it is grounded in relationships, shared purpose and team impact. He appreciates communication that acknowledges people, recognises effort and explains how decisions affect the group. English is not his first language — his native language is Tagalog, which feels more familial and warmer with close co-workers.

Direct or abrupt messages can feel impersonal to Marcus. He contributes enthusiastically when communication feels human, collaborative and psychologically safe.

Step 3 · Create two bespoke personas

Design two fictional personas inspired by communication challenges you've observed at work. Do not base them on specific people, and aim for needs that differ from your own. Processing preferences are situational and task-dependent — not fixed traits or labels.

Direct and output-focused processing
Messages that get to the point, with actions, decisions and deadlines upfront. Outcome clarity reduces cognitive load, especially under time pressure — excess narrative obscures what matters (Locke & Latham 2002).
Sequential and step-based processing
Information broken into ordered steps. Sequencing reduces ambiguity and processing effort for complex or unfamiliar tasks (Sweller 1988).
Structured and patterned processing
Headings, groupings and consistent formats to orient by. Predictable structure improves scanning, prioritisation and validation (Weick 1995).
Contextual and big-picture processing
Background, intent and broader implications first. Contextual framing helps integrate new information with existing mental models — without it, instructions feel arbitrary (Klein 2008).
Detail-sensitive processing
Highly attuned to gaps, assumptions and edge cases. Improves accuracy and risk identification, though it may slow decisions — invaluable in governance, safety, legal and risk contexts (Kahneman 2011).
Relational and tone-sensitive processing
Meaning interpreted strongly through tone, framing and acknowledgement of people. Perceived tone significantly affects trust and willingness to contribute (Edmondson 1999).
Visual or spatial processing
Ideas land more easily represented visually or through metaphor. Combining verbal and visual engages multiple cognitive pathways (Paivio 1991; Mayer 2009).
Language and cultural translation needs
Meaning is shaped by idiom, formality and implicit norms — direct translation often fails to preserve intent or social meaning even when literally accurate (Hall 1976; Hofstede 2011).
No single approach is "better" — effectiveness depends on fit between message form, task demands and cognitive context. Clarity for one person is friction for another.

Your bespoke personas

A sentence or two each: name, role, and how communication lands best for them.

Step 4 · Adapt the message

Rewrite this message for each persona. Keep the meaning intact — adjust tone, structure, emphasis and level of detail only.

The message you will adapt
As part of the Customer Journey Uplift Program, we are preparing an executive report that consolidates the top barriers affecting customer onboarding across all business units. To ensure the report reflects current reality, each business unit lead must provide a prioritised list of no more than five key onboarding issues. Where possible, please include concrete examples, recent data points, or observations from frontline teams.

We are operating within a compressed timeline. Inputs are required by 2 pm Thursday, so the program office can synthesise themes and prepare recommendations for the Executive Leadership Team. Late submissions will not be incorporated into the final report.

Please avoid generic statements such as 'capacity issues' or 'system limitations'. Focus instead on specific friction points your staff and customers experience, such as repeated data re-entry, delays in identity verification, unclear handovers between teams, or inconsistent communication during the onboarding process.

These insights will inform the next phase of discovery, shape resourcing needs, and determine which initiatives will be prioritised in the next funding cycle. If you have questions about scope or need examples of well-defined issues, contact the program office before Wednesday.

Optional — self-insight extension

Identify your own communication preferences, ask AI to rewrite the message for your style, and compare how each version feels to receive. Capture any thought or learning:

Step 5 · Group reflection

  • How did the message feel when tailored to your style?
  • How did it land differently when written for a contrasting persona?
  • What adjustments created clarity, motivation — or confusion?
  • How might this influence how you communicate change or complexity at work?

Facilitator cues & debrief

  • Watch for teams changing the substance of the message rather than structure or tone — redirect.
  • Personas must never represent real individuals.
  • "Clear" communication is situational, not universal — AI accelerates comparison, humans own meaning and appropriateness.
Wellbeing checks

Signalling care, not solving problems.

A wellbeing check is a straightforward message — in person, by email, or in chat — focused on how someone is going: their feelings and relationships, not their work tasks. Sometimes simply knowing someone is available to listen is invaluable, even if no action results. In an AI-driven world, this genuine human connection matters more, not less.

The same wellbeing message can be interpreted many ways — personalising your approach makes it more accessible, respectful and easier to respond to.

Psychological safety matters for all kinds of teams
Interdependent teams need strong relational foundations. A tailored check-in strengthens trust and reduces the risk that someone withdraws or stays silent when they need support.
Hybrid work makes wellbeing less visible
Distributed teams miss small signs of stress or fatigue. Tailoring creates clarity and makes it easier to share honestly without guessing the sender's intent.
Neurodiversity and processing preferences differ
Some people prefer very direct, structured communication; others respond to warm relational language. Tailoring removes ambiguity and reduces cognitive load.
Culture and language shape interpretation
Some cultures acknowledge stress openly; others prefer understatement. A tailored message respects the difference and avoids assumptions about how people "should" communicate.
Workload pressure hides early signals
Teams under pressure normalise overload. A well-framed check-in lets people signal concerns early without feeling exposed or judged.
Example — via email: Hi team, I wanted to take a moment to check in and see how you're going. We've had a lot moving at once recently, and I'm conscious that workloads and priorities can sometimes feel heavy. There's no expectation to respond in detail, but if anything is feeling unclear, overwhelming, or if you'd like support or adjustments, please let me know. If everything is going well, that's great too — this is simply an open door and a reminder that your wellbeing matters.
Example — via chat: Hey Joe — I just wanted to check in with you and say hello. I know we've been in a couple of meetings where things were quite tense, and I wanted you to know you have my support and listening ear should you need it. No need to respond — but don't hesitate to chat any time; we can grab a coffee downstairs.
Activity 3.2Optional — assess for time

Inclusive team wellbeing check-in

Handwrite a check-in first — something you would genuinely send. Then use your LLM to tailor it for different personas, following the human-AI workflow so the final messages stay yours.

Step 1 — your own words first

Draft a typical wellbeing check-in you might send in Slack, Teams or email. Authentic, personable, your own voice. The AI step unlocks when you've written it.

🔒 Locked. Handwrite your check-in first — the first draft should sound like something you would genuinely send. Unlocked. Now tailor with AI — one of the three example personas, and one of your bespoke ones. You keep authorship.
Step 2 — tailoring prompt
Here is my wellbeing check-in message, and a description of the person receiving it: [paste your draft + persona]. Suggest a version tailored to how this person best receives communication. Keep my meaning and warmth. Do not add promises, assumptions, or anything about their performance.

Debrief

  • Wellbeing check-ins are about signalling care, not solving problems.
  • The same message can feel supportive, intrusive or unclear depending on who receives it.
  • Tailoring tone and structure reduces ambiguity and supports psychological safety.

Facilitator cues

  • Ensure the human-AI workflow is followed and participants retain authorship of final messages.
  • Redirect if teams drift toward performance management, HR policy or problem-solving instead of checking in.
