Risks & the Human-Centred AI Workflow

AI brings real benefits into team environments — and three real risks: cognitive offloading, context drift and hallucination. This module names them, shows how they bite, and gives you the repeatable workflow that guards against all three.

Benefits and risks

Two scenarios where AI helps — then quietly hurts.

Both stories start with a genuine productivity win. Reveal each beat and watch where the risk creeps in.

Scenario 1 · The weekly team update

Without AI: team members each write a short update, compare notes, clarify differences and align on the message before sending it upward. Slow — but everyone understands the whole picture.
With AI: the coordinator feeds rough notes and last week's update into an AI tool. A clean draft appears quickly — less writing time, less duplication, consistent tone. A genuine win.
The risk: because the draft feels finished, people spend less time checking facts or calibrating with each other. Priority shifts discussed verbally but never written down fail to appear. Subtle risks are missed.
The result: the update looks polished, but the team's understanding is thinner — and the misalignment surfaces in the leadership meeting. This is cognitive offloading.

Scenario 2 · The client briefing pack

Without AI: a consulting team reads through discovery notes, highlights important details and debates which themes should anchor the deck. Slow — but they know the evidence cold.
With AI: the lead consultant uploads transcripts, emails and whiteboard photos into a summarisation tool. Neat themes and draft slide text appear — faster early stages, less noise, coherent structure.
The risk: because the output feels comprehensive, the team examines the raw material less. Contradictions across interviews get flattened. A small but high-impact customer segment disappears entirely.
The result: the client queries the basis of an insight — and the team can't point to the source. Well-structured deck, weak grasp of the evidence, confidence drops in the room. This is context drift.
Risk 1

Cognitive offloading — what the research shows.

Offloading happens when a tool holds, processes or interprets information you would normally retain — AI drafting, rewriting or structuring before a human has fully processed the material. The MIT Media Lab's Your Brain on ChatGPT study (54 participants, EEG-tracked writing tasks) measured what that costs:

For teams, the implications are direct: individuals remember less of the underlying detail; reduced ownership weakens accountability for recommendations; frequent AI use across a day can degrade later tasks; and teams that skip verification miss risks, nuance and contextual meaning.

"AI assistance changes how people engage with their own ideas, affecting both memory and commitment to the work." — MIT Media Lab, Your Brain on ChatGPT, 2024.
Risk 2

Context drift.

Unlike humans, AI systems work within a fixed "window" of text. As conversations lengthen — typically seven or more rounds of prompts — earlier information gets condensed or dropped to make room for new input. Specific details fade, subtle nuances disappear, and the model's grasp of your context quietly degrades.

Relying on drifted outputs — summaries, recommendations, briefing packs — means missing vital earlier detail, especially when nobody cross-checks against the original source material.

The counter-move: deliberately re-inject earlier prompts and restate key details as the interaction lengthens. Reintroducing important information maintains continuity and keeps the AI anchored to your actual context.
Risk 3

Hallucinations — confident, plausible, wrong.

A hallucination is AI-generated information that appears confident and plausible but is factually incorrect, invented, or unsupported by the material provided. In teamwork it's dangerous because fabricated details enter documents, decisions and discussions without anyone noticing.

Why they happen

  • Training data lacks context and nuance — under-represented languages, paywalled domains, true expert-level knowledge. The model answers with "the average intelligence of the internet".
  • Neural nets are probabilistic, not rule-based — identical inputs won't produce identical outputs.
  • LLMs don't "look up" the internet when answering, and have limited working memory.
  • Models are built at a point in time — without prompting them to fetch recent information, they can't know breaking news.

When they bite hardest

  • Prompts lack context.
  • The AI is asked to infer beyond what it knows.
  • Verification steps are skipped.
  • Cognitive offloading is already in play — a less-engaged team is far less likely to catch the invention.

The answer — used for the rest of today

An ethical, human-centric AI workflow

Six steps that get the benefits of AI without the drawbacks: verify outputs, re-inject context deliberately, and keep humans owning meaning, decision logic and communication quality.

Break the task into smaller steps

The AI may assist with suggestions, but the list of steps and the approach are guided by the human.

Prompt one step at a time

Small, scoped prompts keep you in control and make errors visible early.

Validate each output

Check every step before proceeding — meaning drift, added content, lost nuance.

Re-inject or refine

When accuracy declines, reintroduce your original notes and restate key context.

Final human review

Review the complete output — content-complete is not the same as usable.

Rewrite in your own tone

Re-writing in your own words strengthens recall, ownership and your natural voice.

Why this works

  • Reduces cognitive offloading — the human stays actively engaged at each stage.
  • Improves accuracy — errors are corrected before they compound across a task.
  • Supports ethical use — the human remains responsible for meaning, judgement and decision quality.
  • Produces clearer thinking — breaking work into steps reveals assumptions and gaps in logic.
  • Strengthens accountability — the final output reflects human authorship, not unexamined AI suggestions.
