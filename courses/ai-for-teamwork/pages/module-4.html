Module 4

Optimise Time Together

Meetings are how teams coordinate, share information and decide. This module uses AI before, during and after: clarify purpose before you convene, turn transcripts into accountable actions, and let AI challenge your assumptions before you commit.

🕘 ~50 minutes · Activities 4.1, 4.2 & 4.3
Why it matters

Why optimising meetings empowers teams.

Much of the time organisations lose in meetings stems from unclear agendas, uneven participation and fragmented context entering the room (Allen, Lehmann-Willenbrock & Rogelberg 2018). High-performing teams counter this with shared preparation, context alignment and clarity of purpose — the three factors repeatedly identified as the strongest predictors of meeting effectiveness.

Meetings run poorly when people arrive with different assumptions, incomplete context or unclear expectations. Without a shared understanding of purpose, discussions drift and decisions are hard to capture. Fragmented notes then make it hard to identify what was decided, what remains unresolved and who owns next steps. AI helps by organising information before the meeting, making assumptions visible, and transforming raw notes into decisions and actions — but only with verification and human judgement.

Optimised meetings create: stronger direction, less rework, and better team efficiency and shared understanding.
The technique

The knowledge file — anchoring AI to what you actually know.

A knowledge file is a short collection of information you prepare before working with an LLM. It gives the model the context you already hold — improving accuracy, reducing drift, preventing hallucinations, and keeping you in control of meaning. You'll use them for the rest of the course.

To create one

  • List what you already know about the situation.
  • Capture facts, constraints, risks and unanswered questions.
  • Describe who is involved and why their perspectives matter.
  • Add relevant timelines, pressures or decisions due.
  • Keep it concise and factual.
  • Save it to a PDF and re-import it into your LLM (method varies by tool).
Why it works: a knowledge file is a living artefact — it improves alignment before, during and after the meeting, and every prompt that references it starts from your verified context instead of the model's guesses.
Activity 4.1

Preparing for and optimising a meeting

The scenario below is deliberately ambiguous — exactly the situation where an unguided AI will happily "fill in" the gaps with false assumptions. Separate what is known, inferred and missing before you prompt anything.

Teams message · Wednesday 4:52 pm · Rachel T. (your manager)
"Can you pull together a meeting with the project teams so we can get on the same page before things slip further? There are concerns. Let's regroup early next week."
She goes offline almost immediately to join another call. No further detail follows.
Recent background — the Orion program
Your organisation is midway through a multi-stream transformation program called Orion, with three delivery workstreams that must land together for a Q3 launch. Over the past two months:
  • The delivery schedule has been re-baselined twice due to a vendor onboarding delay.
  • The integration team raised concerns about API readiness, but no consolidated impact assessment exists.
  • Requirements sign-off has produced contradictory decisions made verbally across channels.
  • The change team is fielding inconsistent messages from senior leaders.
  • Delivery and Product have been escalating concerns independently — neither aware of the other's escalation.
  • A senior executive informally asked for "a clear narrative of where things actually are".
Internal Slack hints at growing frustration: engineers unclear why priorities shifted twice this week; three decision points awaiting Product Owner confirmation; the dashboard shows "amber" but some feel it should be "red"; a customer demo was postponed with no shared rationale.
What happened this morning
At stand-up, two developers said they were "blocked by unconfirmed requirements" — while the BA believed those requirements were already approved. Meanwhile the Change Manager heard that stakeholders across Operations "do not understand what is changing next month", even though no communications had been planned for next month. None of these signals have been consolidated into a single view.
Your manager — Rachel T.
Senior Program Manager, six years with the organisation. Known for structured thinking, calm leadership and evidence-based decisions; background in large-scale banking transformation, stabilising programs drifting into ambiguity. Values concise reliable escalation, cross-team collaboration, clear narrative, and people who bring forward well-organised information. Respected but stretched — pulled into governance forums for three weeks with less direct visibility of delivery. Which makes her short, vague, urgent message stand out even more.
Possible attendees
Sarah N.Delivery LeadOversees timelines and progress. Needs clarity on emerging risks and blocked dependencies.
Alex T.Business AnalystManages requirements. Needs to know what changed and what requires revalidation.
Daniel R.Product OwnerOwns prioritisation and customer value. Needs release implications and trade-offs.
Priya N.Technical LeadAssesses integration risks. Needs specifics about what is slipping and why.
Marcus L.Change & Comms ManagerNeeds a coherent narrative about what happened and what comes next.

Before any prompting, triage. Tap a statement, then tap its column.

Steps 2–5 · Design the meeting, build the knowledge file, confirm with Rachel

Discussion questions

  • Where could AI help you surface clarity faster — without deciding anything for you?
  • What part of this would you not trust AI with, and why?
  • What would you explicitly check or rewrite before sharing anything with others?

Facilitator cues & debrief

  • Watch for teams jumping to scheduling before clarifying purpose and outcomes — redirect.
  • The knowledge file is a living artefact — before, during and after the meeting.
  • This activity surfaces how quickly teams default to action when clarity is missing.
After the meeting

Transcripts and raw notes → decisions and actions.

Post-meeting notes are usually disorganised and incomplete. An LLM can group related points, highlight key decisions, surface unresolved questions and organise actionable steps — and blending its synthesis with your own notes and trackers builds a more complete picture. As always: review and confirm that nothing has been misrepresented or fabricated.

Activity 4.2

Turning a meeting transcript into useful summaries

One AI-transcribed meeting, three audiences. Follow the human-AI loop — one step at a time, validating at each step — and produce three clearly different summaries. Download the transcript or copy it below.

View / copy the sample transcript (Customer Portal Relaunch — weekly sync)
AI-generated transcript — may contain transcription errors
Meeting: Customer Portal Relaunch — weekly project sync (32 min)
Attendees: Priya (project lead), Marcus (engineering), Lena (design), Tom (marketing), Aisha (customer support). Apologies: Daniel (finance).

Priya: Quick round on the portal relaunch. Marcus, where are we on the login migration?
Marcus: The migration script passed staging on Tuesday. There's still the edge case with accounts created before 2019 — maybe two thousand users, could be more, I haven't pulled the exact number.
Priya: Is that a blocker for the the fifteenth?
Marcus: Not if those users get the reset email flow. But someone needs to sign that off — not an engineering call.
Aisha: Support would want at least a week's notice before reset emails go out. Last time we got hammered with tickets.
Priya: Noted. I'll take the sign-off to Sandra. [inaudible] probably Thursday.
Lena: Design update — dashboard screens done except billing. Waiting on final copy from Tom's team.
Tom: Copy's basically done, legal just needs to glance at the refund wording. Back by Friday, worst case Monday.
Lena: Monday still makes the build cut-off, but nothing after Monday please.
Marcus: One more thing — performance. We deferred the caching layer to phase two, but load tests show the search page at four seconds on peak profile. Worse than the current site.
Priya: Four seconds is not shippable. What would a fix take inside this phase?
Marcus: Rough guess, three or four days. It would push the code freeze. Or ship with search as-is flagged as known — I wouldn't recommend it.
Tom: Launching slower than the old site is the one thing the press release can't survive.
Priya: Agreed — provisionally take the three to four days. Marcus, confirm the estimate by tomorrow midday and we'll decide whether the fifteenth moves.
Aisha: Help articles — eleven of fourteen drafted; the last three depend on final billing screens.
Lena: I can give you near-final screenshots now.
Priya: Tom, the email announcement?
Tom: Draft's with brand. Teaser on the tenth, main announcement launch morning. Rachel asked about the partner newsletter — I said I'd find out.
Priya: Partners go in the main send, not the teaser. Yours, Tom. Risks — anything new?
Marcus: Daniel flagged the invoice-export vendor renewal lands mid-month — if procurement slips, exports could be offline at launch. Low likelihood, ugly if it happens.
Priya: Aisha, you talk to that vendor already — chase it? Status by Wednesday.
Aisha: Can do.
Priya: Decisions so far: performance fix provisionally in, billing copy Friday-slash-Monday, partners in main announcement only. Still open: the pre-2019 account approach (with me and Sandra), and whether the launch date moves, pending Marcus's estimate.
Lena: The old portal's green "beta feedback" tab — keeping it? Looks dated but people use it.
Priya: Keep for launch, review usage in a month. Same time next week: Marcus midday tomorrow on the estimate, Tom partner comms, Aisha vendor status Wednesday, me on the Sandra sign-off.
The human-AI loop, again: break the task into steps · prompt one step at a time · validate each output · re-inject when accuracy declines · final human review · rewrite in your own tone. The human sets the steps — even if AI suggests options.

Debrief

  • Summaries sound plausible even when they are wrong.
  • Audience tailoring increases usefulness — and the risk of accidental invention.
  • Validation at each step reduces drift across the three versions.
Activity 4.3

Challenging assumptions to strengthen your decision

AI can broaden perspectives, surface assumptions and expose risks — as a challenger, not a decision-maker. Reach your own conclusions first; the AI step unlocks after you've committed to them.

The scenario — a flagship capability program losing steam
Scenario
You are leading a major internal capability program launched as a flagship initiative by the executive team — building core skills in data, AI and modern ways of working across multiple business units, positioned as a key enabler of strategy. The first two waves were very successful: strong attendance, positive feedback, visible executive sponsorship.

Over the past four weeks, registrations have declined, attendance has fallen, and engagement has weakened — people arrive late, keep cameras off, contribute minimally. A few managers hint informally that staff feel overloaded and see the program as an "extra" on top of real work, but nobody has raised this formally. Leadership still calls the program non-negotiable and expects full scope. They want a revised participation strategy by next week.

You do not have clear data on which groups are disengaging or why. There are no agreed metrics beyond attendance and basic satisfaction, and no shared view on whether the cause is timing, relevance, facilitation style, workload, or a combination. Some stakeholders privately suggest managers aren't giving people protected time. Others imply the content feels too generic. You must decide whether to continue, reshape or temporarily slow the program — and how to position that with executives.

Many assumptions risk shaping your thinking: that low attendance equals low interest (rather than workload conflict); that executive sponsorship translates into local manager support; that the current format is the only viable format; that people who attend are actually engaged. Your decision affects staff workload, program credibility, and your own reputation with leadership.

Step 2 — your conclusions first

Discuss in your team. What do you recommend, and why? Commit before consulting AI — the challenge step unlocks when you have.

🔒 Locked. Record your own conclusions first — AI challenges thinking; it doesn't replace it. Unlocked. Now let the AI attack your reasoning — and judge which challenges actually matter.
Step 3 — challenge prompt
Here is a scenario and my team's conclusions: [paste both]. Challenge our thinking. Give us alternative interpretations, disagree with our conclusion where the evidence allows, and name the assumptions we appear to be making. Do not soften your critique.

Discussion questions

  • Which assumptions were you most confident in before using AI?
  • Which assumptions did the AI challenge that you had not explicitly named?
  • Where did the critique feel useful — and where misaligned with context or reality?

Debrief & cues

  • Assumptions sit beneath confident recommendations.
  • AI is a challenger, not a decision-maker; human judgement decides which challenges matter.
  • Redirect if teams accept AI feedback without disagreement or qualification.
